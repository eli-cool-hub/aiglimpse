FTC Chair: Developers Bear Liability for AI Systems, Not the Tools

Federal regulators reject framing autonomous AI agents as independent legal actors, insisting human creators remain accountable.

By AI Glimpse Newsroom
·
September 26, 2026

The Federal Trade Commission's leadership is drawing a firm regulatory line: artificial intelligence systems cannot escape responsibility by claiming autonomy. According to AI Weekly, FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson used a keynote appearance at the Reuters Momentum AI conference in Austin to push back against industry efforts to treat AI agents as independent actors shielded from liability.

Ferguson rejected the language tech companies increasingly use to describe their systems as self-governing entities. "I'm going to continue as long as I am chairman to resist this anthropomorphizing of these tools," he stated during the Reuters NEXT newsmaker interview. The chairman's position suggests the regulatory agency plans to maintain its stance that responsibility for AI-driven harms flows back to the humans and organizations that developed and deployed them.

The Liability Question

Ferguson framed the accountability debate using a straightforward analogy: "the man who wielded the hammer ought to suffer the consequences," he said. The comparison underscores the FTC's view that developers, companies, and decision-makers who direct AI systems bear primary responsibility for outcomes, regardless of how sophisticated or autonomous the technology appears.

This regulatory posture matters significantly as AI systems become more prevalent in commerce, healthcare, employment, and consumer services. If courts or regulators were to treat advanced AI agents as legal actors capable of independent decision-making, companies could potentially shield themselves from liability by claiming their systems acted without authorization or guidance. Ferguson's remarks suggest the FTC intends to close that loophole.

Industry Pushback on Framing

Technology companies have increasingly adopted language describing their AI systems in humanistic terms. Phrases like "autonomous agents," "self-directed models," and "independent decision-makers" have crept into product marketing and technical documentation. This terminology serves dual purposes: it appeals to investors by suggesting advanced capabilities, and it potentially creates legal distance between corporate entities and algorithmic outcomes.

Ferguson's comments signal that regulators view this linguistic shift with skepticism. The FTC chair's resistance suggests that regardless of how companies market or describe their AI systems, the agency will evaluate accountability through a lens that prioritizes human oversight and responsibility.

What's at Stake

  • Companies deploying AI systems could face direct liability for discriminatory outcomes, fraud, or consumer harm
  • The regulatory framework could require explicit human accountability chains for AI-driven decisions
  • Tech companies may need to maintain detailed audit trails and decision logs for their AI systems
  • Insurance and compliance strategies may need to shift toward accepting rather than deflecting responsibility

Ferguson's position reflects a broader regulatory concern that treating AI as an autonomous actor could erode consumer protections and corporate accountability. As artificial intelligence systems increasingly make consequential decisions affecting people's lives, regulators are signaling they will not allow the technology's sophistication to become an excuse for regulatory evasion.

The FTC's stance could reshape how companies architect, deploy, and insure their AI systems. Rather than pursuing technological autonomy as a design goal, organizations may need to prioritize transparency, auditability, and human oversight as compliance requirements.
