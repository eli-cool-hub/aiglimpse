New York City Moves to Restrict Facial Recognition in Public Spaces

A proposed law gaining momentum in the City Council would ban facial scanning technology in venues open to the public, marking a significant shift in AI regulation.

By AI Glimpse Newsroom
·
August 16, 2026

New York City is poised to become a leader in restricting how businesses deploy facial recognition technology. According to AI Weekly, a City Council bill sponsored by Council Member Shahana Hanif has secured backing from the mayor's administration and a majority of council members, signaling potential passage of what proponents call the 'Ban the Scan' legislation.

The measure, officially designated as Int. 213, would prohibit publicly accessible businesses from using facial recognition systems to identify or scan customers without explicit consent. The proposal has garnered support from 28 council co-sponsors, surpassing the 26 votes needed to pass in the 51-member body. Samuel A.A. Levine, commissioner of the Department of Consumer and Worker Protection, has added the administration's weight behind the effort.

What Prompted the Push

The legislation emerged from growing concerns about how facial recognition technology is being deployed at high-profile venues. Madison Square Garden has become the focal point of the debate, with critics raising alarms about the arena's use of facial identification systems to identify and deny entry to certain individuals, including individuals involved in legal disputes with the venue's operators.

The convergence of these issues has created unusual political alignment. Consumer protection advocates, privacy organizations, and elected officials across ideological lines have expressed concern about the lack of guardrails governing facial scanning in commercial settings.

Implications for AI Deployment

The proposed ban reflects broader anxiety about how artificial intelligence tools are being integrated into everyday infrastructure without adequate public input or regulatory frameworks. Facial recognition systems, powered by machine learning algorithms trained on large datasets, have become increasingly accurate and affordable, leading to rapid adoption across retail, hospitality, and entertainment venues.

The New York City bill addresses a specific gap in current privacy law. While some jurisdictions have restricted government use of facial recognition, fewer have targeted private sector deployment in public-facing environments. The measure would establish a new standard for how companies must handle biometric data collection.

Next Steps

  • The bill requires passage by the full City Council before advancing to the mayor's desk
  • If signed into law, it would take effect 180 days after enactment
  • Businesses in violation could face fines and other penalties
  • The legislation may prompt similar efforts in other major cities

The momentum behind Int. 213 suggests that cities are beginning to take seriously the governance challenges posed by AI systems that operate at scale. Whether New York's approach becomes a model for other municipalities remains to be seen, but the political coalition supporting this measure indicates that concerns about facial recognition have moved beyond activist circles into mainstream policymaking.
