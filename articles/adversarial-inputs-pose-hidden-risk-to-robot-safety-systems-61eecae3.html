Adversarial Inputs Pose Hidden Risk to Robot Safety Systems

Security researchers reveal how manipulated sensor data can bypass the protective mechanisms designed to keep robots from harming humans.

By AI Glimpse Newsroom
·
October 27, 2026

The safety systems protecting industrial robots from collisions and accidents rely on a fundamental assumption: their sensor inputs are trustworthy. New research challenges that assumption, demonstrating that deliberately crafted false data can circumvent the very safeguards meant to prevent harm.

According to IEEE Spectrum AI, security teams at VicOne LAB R7 have documented how manipulated inputs can alter robot behavior in ways that bypass existing protective layers. In one test, researchers injected a false message into a robot expected to remain stationary under safety constraints, causing it to move unexpectedly. In another scenario, text displayed on a poster caused a robot dog using a vision-language-action model to deviate from its assigned task.

The implications extend beyond individual robots executing wrong commands. Protective systems themselves, designed to intervene when danger approaches, depend on sensor readings and status signals to know when to activate. If an attacker can poison those same inputs, the safety layer may never trigger.

Redundancy Is Not Automatically Sufficient

Engineers often deploy multiple sensors specifically to catch failures. A robot might use both cameras and LiDAR to perceive obstacles, reasoning that both sensors would need to fail simultaneously for a collision to occur. Recent research on autonomous vehicles reveals a critical flaw in that logic: a single crafted object in the environment can fool both systems at once.

This matters for collaborative robots working alongside humans, mobile platforms navigating facilities, and humanoid systems in hospitals. Each relies on sensor fusion to make decisions about speed, stopping distance, and motion. An adversarial attack that affects multiple sensors simultaneously transforms what seemed like an acceptable safety margin into a genuine vulnerability.

Intent Changes Risk Assessment

Traditional safety engineering accounts for random component failures, environmental sensing errors, and cascading malfunctions. These events follow statistical patterns. A fault occurs once, and backup systems catch it. Deliberate cyber attacks operate differently.

An attacker can choose the precise moment to introduce false data, repeat the same manipulation consistently, and escalate timing or intensity based on system response. This transforms low-probability accident scenarios into reproducible attack sequences. A failure combination that safety analysis deemed acceptable when treated as accidental becomes unacceptable when treated as adversarial.

Security Testing Now Part of Safety Validation

The research reveals a critical gap in how manufacturers validate robot safety. Standard protocols test how robots respond to component failures and measurement uncertainty. They rarely test how robots respond to adversarial inputs crafted to bypass those protections.

  • Sensor manipulation through acoustic attacks targeting gyroscopes
  • Visual tricks exploiting vision-language model vulnerabilities
  • Message injection in robot operating systems like ROS 2/DDS
  • Coordinated attacks affecting multiple sensors simultaneously

Addressing these risks requires integrating adversarial security testing into safety validation workflows. Teams must verify that protective systems detect threats independently, rather than depending on the same potentially compromised sensor streams that could be manipulated. The gap between safe-by-design and secure-by-design has become impossible to ignore as robots increasingly operate near humans in shared spaces.
