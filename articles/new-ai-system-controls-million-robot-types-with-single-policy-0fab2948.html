New AI System Controls Million Robot Types With Single Policy

Researchers demonstrate how machine learning can adapt a unified control system across vastly different robot morphologies by embedding kinematic understanding.

By AI Glimpse Newsroom
·
November 24, 2026

A team of researchers has achieved a significant breakthrough in robot control by training a single artificial intelligence policy capable of commanding robots with fundamentally different physical structures. The advance addresses a longstanding challenge in embodied AI: creating flexible control systems that generalize across diverse hardware without requiring separate training for each robot type.

The system, called OmniAct, represents a departure from previous approaches that relied on abstract action representations. Rather than learning generic motion primitives, OmniAct conditions its behavior directly on each robot's specific kinematic properties. According to arXiv, the method encodes information about joint positions, motion axes, and how the robot's limbs connect to its sensors, allowing the policy to reason about which movements are physically feasible and effective given the embodiment's particular constraints.

Training at Scale

To develop this capability, the researchers procedurally generated and validated one million physically plausible robot designs. They then developed algorithms to automatically generate manipulation trajectories for all embodiments within simulation environments. This massive synthetic dataset enabled training across numerous manipulation tasks without requiring real-world robot access.

The technical foundation uses flow-matching, a generative modeling technique that predicts relative motions for individual joints. This approach naturally accommodates robots with different numbers of degrees of freedom and varying kinematic structures, from industrial arms to anthropomorphic hands.

Empirical Performance

Evaluation results demonstrate substantial advantages over existing methods:

  • On held-out test scenarios involving unseen embodiment-scene combinations, OmniAct achieved 63.0% task success compared to 17.8% for a leading baseline
  • When evaluated on task families used to test competing systems, OmniAct reached 64.7% success versus 41.1% for GPT-6 Astra
  • On real-world hardware not encountered during training, the system achieved nearly double the success rate of the strongest alternative approach

Beyond raw performance metrics, researchers observed that OmniAct learned morphology-specific manipulation strategies. Rather than applying identical techniques universally, the policy adapted tactics to exploit each robot's particular strengths and work around its limitations. This suggests the system develops genuine understanding of how physical form constrains and enables different manipulation approaches.

Implications for Robotics

The breakthrough carries important implications for robot deployment and development. Rather than requiring specialized policies for each hardware configuration, practitioners could potentially leverage a unified model across their robotic fleet. This could accelerate adoption of robotic systems in environments where multiple embodiment types operate alongside one another.

The work also demonstrates that artificial intelligence systems trained on procedurally generated embodiments can transfer effectively to real hardware, suggesting viable pathways for scaling robot learning without exhaustive real-world data collection. The researchers have made their project details publicly available for further community development.
