Schiff: Voluntary AI Pledges Insufficient Without Formal Regulation

California senator argues tech industry self-governance fails to address AI safety risks, calls for dedicated regulatory agency.

By AI Glimpse Newsroom
·
October 5, 2026

Senator Adam Schiff of California, who serves on multiple congressional committees overseeing technology policy, has raised serious concerns about the adequacy of voluntary commitments from artificial intelligence companies, arguing that self-regulation cannot substitute for binding legal frameworks.

The senator's critique centers on a recent White House gathering where technology executives signed a non-binding pledge to evaluate their own AI systems for safety and security risks. According to The Verge, Schiff noted a fundamental contradiction: the same company leaders publicly acknowledge the need for formal regulation while simultaneously endorsing voluntary measures.

"It was interesting to see all those tech leaders there signing onto this voluntary pledge on the one hand and on the other hand, away from the White House grounds, away from the president, being very public and saying, 'We need regulation. We don't want this to be just Trust us, we'll do the right thing.'"

The Regulatory Gap

Schiff's position reflects growing frustration among lawmakers about the disconnect between industry rhetoric and actual governance mechanisms. The voluntary pledge approach has become standard practice in Silicon Valley, yet these non-binding commitments have repeatedly failed to prevent harmful outcomes in social media, algorithmic recommendation systems, and data privacy.

The senator sits on committees addressing intellectual property, antitrust enforcement, privacy protections, and technology oversight. From this vantage point, he has observed how the current regulatory environment leaves significant gaps in AI oversight, particularly regarding emerging risks that existing frameworks were never designed to address.

National Security as Justification

Schiff identified an additional layer of complexity: the administration's tendency to invoke national security concerns selectively. While invoking security rationales to expand executive authority in various contexts, the administration has declined to impose mandatory AI safety standards despite widespread acknowledgment that advanced systems pose existential risks.

This selective application of national security doctrine undermines the credibility of security-based arguments and raises questions about the actual priority afforded to AI governance within executive branch policymaking.

Path Forward

The senator advocates for establishing a dedicated federal agency with explicit authority to regulate artificial intelligence development and deployment. This approach would create clear legal standards, enforcement mechanisms, and accountability structures rather than relying on industry self-assessment.

  • Formal regulatory frameworks with binding requirements
  • Dedicated agency with enforcement authority
  • Legal standards replacing voluntary pledges
  • Congressional oversight mechanisms

Schiff's position aligns with calls from prominent AI researchers and safety advocates who argue that market-based solutions and voluntary compliance have proven inadequate for managing technological risks at scale. The challenge remains significant: any new regulatory structure would face immediate legal challenges, particularly following recent Supreme Court decisions limiting agency deference in cases involving statutory ambiguity.
