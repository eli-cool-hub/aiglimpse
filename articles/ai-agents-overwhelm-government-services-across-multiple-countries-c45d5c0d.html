AI Agents Overwhelm Government Services Across Multiple Countries

Research documents surge in automated requests flooding housing, financial, and judicial systems worldwide.

By AI Glimpse Newsroom
·
September 10, 2026

Autonomous AI systems are creating an unexpected strain on public institutions, according to emerging research that tracks a phenomenon researchers have termed "agentic flooding." Across multiple nations, government agencies handling citizen complaints and petitions are experiencing unprecedented surges in volume that correlate directly with the proliferation of large language model tools like ChatGPT.

According to AI Weekly, Chris Schmitz from the Centre for the Governance of AI and the Hertie School has documented 84 distinct instances of this pattern across 11 countries. His team's analysis, published to arXiv in August, reveals a consistent trend: automated AI systems are submitting vastly more requests to public services than human users previously did.

The Numbers Tell a Stark Story

The data paints a troubling picture for already-stretched government resources. The UK housing ombudsman received 7,000 complaints last year, more than double the 2,600 filed in 2022. The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau experienced even sharper growth, with complaints rising roughly five times over the same period. Similar escalation patterns emerged in Brazil's judicial petition systems and Germany's parliamentary correspondence channels.

Schmitz's research suggests this is not simply increased consumer activism. Instead, the surge appears driven by AI agents programmed to submit appeals, complaints, and formal requests on behalf of their users, often without meaningful human review or consideration of whether individual submissions have merit.

Why This Matters for Policy and Infrastructure

The implications extend beyond inconvenience. Public institutions designed to process complaints and petitions from human citizens face unexpected computational and administrative challenges when confronted with volume increases of this magnitude. Staff accustomed to managing thousands of annual requests must suddenly contend with tens of thousands, straining both personnel and existing systems.

  • Government agencies lack clear protocols for distinguishing between legitimate human complaints and automated submissions
  • Current oversight mechanisms assume a human decision-maker reviews requests before filing
  • Institutions may need new legal frameworks to handle AI-generated petitions

The research also highlights a governance gap. Many AI agents operate with minimal transparency regarding the requests they file on users' behalf. Citizens may not fully understand that their AI tools are submitting formal complaints, potentially creating redundancy and cluttering official channels with duplicate or questionable submissions.

What Comes Next

Schmitz's documentation of agentic flooding arrives as governments worldwide grapple with AI regulation. The phenomenon suggests that policymakers need not only consider AI's capacity to generate misinformation or cause harm, but also its mundane ability to simply overwhelm existing institutions through volume.

Some jurisdictions may implement rate-limiting for automated submissions. Others might require AI developers to implement verification protocols before filing formal requests. The European Union, which has taken a leading role in AI governance, could address this through its AI Act framework.

The underlying problem remains unresolved: as AI tools become more capable and widespread, they operate within institutional environments designed for human-scale interaction. Without deliberate intervention, agentic flooding may become a chronic challenge for public services seeking to remain accessible and responsive.
