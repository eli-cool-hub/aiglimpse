AI Regulation Risk: Gatekeeping Over Safety, Experts Warn

Policy frameworks designed to manage AI capabilities could entrench incumbent power rather than protect public interests.

By AI Glimpse Newsroom
·
June 28, 2026

The conversation around artificial intelligence regulation is at a critical juncture, with policy experts raising concerns that safety-focused frameworks may inadvertently concentrate market power among the largest technology companies.

According to AI Weekly, the framing of regulatory proposals carries substantial weight in determining their real-world impact. Rather than focusing narrowly on containing the risks posed by increasingly autonomous systems, current policy discussions have begun to center on a different question entirely: who gets to control access to the most advanced models.

Access Control as Regulatory Tool

This shift in emphasis creates a distinction between regulation designed to mitigate genuine technological risks and regulation that functions as a competitive moat. Consider the unnamed frontier models emerging from organizations like OpenAI. If regulatory frameworks require government approval for deployment or impose licensing requirements on model access, the practical effect may favor companies with existing regulatory relationships and substantial legal resources.

Small businesses and independent developers face immediate and concrete competitive disadvantages under such regimes. A startup operating in this space cannot easily absorb the compliance costs associated with complex permitting processes. Larger incumbents, by contrast, can absorb these expenses as a cost of doing business while simultaneously using them to discourage new entrants.

The Safety vs. Control Distinction

The critical question facing policymakers is whether emerging regulations genuinely address safety concerns or whether they primarily serve to concentrate market control. These outcomes are not mutually exclusive: a rule could accomplish both goals simultaneously.

  • Genuine safety measures address specific, identifiable harms from AI systems
  • Access restrictions may follow safety justifications while primarily protecting incumbent market position
  • Smaller developers and research teams lack resources to navigate complex approval processes
  • Regulatory capture occurs when industry shapes rules designed to oversee it

The Stakes for Competition

The consolidation of AI capability behind a small number of well-capitalized firms has implications beyond market competition. Diverse development ecosystems typically produce stronger innovation, varied approaches to safety and reliability, and healthy technical debate. When regulatory barriers limit who can participate, the entire field suffers from reduced competition between different architectural approaches and methodologies.

Policymakers must distinguish between rules that address genuine harms and rules that function primarily as gatekeeping mechanisms protecting incumbent advantages.

Observers of AI policy should monitor whether proposed regulations explicitly tie restrictions to measurable safety outcomes or whether they instead impose broad access controls justified by safety concerns. The distinction matters enormously for the trajectory of the industry and the distribution of power within it.

This dynamic reflects a broader pattern in technology regulation: frameworks nominally designed to manage risk often consolidate power among actors who can afford compliance costs. The AI sector now faces the same choice that previous technology waves confronted: build regulatory systems that genuinely address public concerns while preserving competitive dynamism, or accept frameworks that sacrifice competition in the name of control.
