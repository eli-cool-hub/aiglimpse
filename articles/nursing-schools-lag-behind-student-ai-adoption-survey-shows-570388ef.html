Nursing Schools Lag Behind Student AI Adoption, Survey Shows

As students rapidly integrate AI into their studies, academic programs struggle to develop policies and training for the technology already reshaping clinical education.

By AI Glimpse Newsroom
·
November 6, 2026

A significant gap has emerged between how quickly nursing students are embracing artificial intelligence and the pace at which educational institutions are developing governance frameworks around its use. According to Becker's Hospital Review, a new survey by Wolters Kluwer reveals that the majority of nursing trainees are already using AI tools in their coursework, while faculty members scramble to establish guidelines for a technology that has already become embedded in student workflows.

The divergence is striking. Nearly 4 in 5 Bachelor of Science in Nursing students reported using AI tools, compared to 77 percent of Licensed Practical Nurse students and 66 percent of Associate Degree in Nursing students. Newer students show particularly high adoption rates, reflecting their exposure to rapidly expanding AI capabilities. When accounting for frequency, the engagement runs deep: one quarter of students use these tools twice weekly or more, while one fifth incorporate them into their daily study routines.

Students See AI as Enhancement, Not Replacement

The way nursing students deploy AI reflects practical thinking about its role in their education. About half use the technology to support studying, test their knowledge retention, and prepare for licensing exams. Critically, students view AI as a supplement to traditional instruction rather than a substitute for it. However, they anticipate significantly expanded usage: students project that 66 percent will rely on AI for studying within the next year, up from the current 53 percent.

Despite widespread adoption, significant barriers remain. The leading concern cited by 57 percent of students is fundamental doubt about accuracy. One third worry that unapproved tools violate school policies, while roughly 28 percent each fear that AI reliance could weaken clinical reasoning skills or lack sufficient understanding of the technology's capabilities and limitations. Data security concerns round out the major obstacles.

Faculty Training Lags Behind Classroom Reality

The institutional response has been slow and fragmented. A separate study published in October 2025 in the Journal of Nursing Regulation found that only 24 percent of registered nurse programs have incorporated generative AI into their curriculum, and just 17 percent teach dedicated coursework on AI or digital health topics. Over three quarters of programs either avoid using AI entirely or remain uncertain about how to integrate it meaningfully.

Limited faculty preparation emerges as the primary constraint. Most educators lack the training and confidence necessary to guide student AI use effectively, leaving them unable to establish clear expectations or demonstrate best practices.

Healthcare Systems Are Already Moving Forward

The challenge becomes more urgent when considering the professional environment awaiting these graduates. Major health systems including Stanford Medicine and Tampa General Hospital have already deployed AI tools such as ambient documentation systems into nursing workflows. Mercy is actively developing nurse-focused documentation software powered by AI. This means today's students will enter clinical practice in environments where artificial intelligence is a standard operational component, not an emerging novelty.

To address the acceleration gap, researchers recommend that nursing programs invest substantially in faculty development, embed AI instruction within existing coursework rather than leaving adoption to external tools, emphasize clinical applications, and establish explicit policies governing ethical use and academic integrity. Without deliberate action, schools risk training nurses whose education did not adequately prepare them for the AI-integrated care settings they will enter professionally.

Based on reporting from Becker's Hospital Review.
