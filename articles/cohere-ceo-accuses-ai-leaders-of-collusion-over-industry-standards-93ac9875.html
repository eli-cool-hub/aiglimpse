Cohere CEO Accuses AI Leaders of Collusion Over Industry Standards

Gomez claims major tech firms are seeking regulatory exemptions to coordinate AI development, raising fresh antitrust concerns.

By AI Glimpse Newsroom
·
September 14, 2026

Cohere's leadership is raising alarm about what it characterizes as anticompetitive behavior among the industry's largest players, alleging that a handful of dominant Silicon Valley artificial intelligence companies are attempting to establish a standards-setting regime that would effectively function as a cartel. According to AI Weekly, the accusations come from co-founder and CEO Aidan Gomez, who outlined his concerns in a September 13 essay published on Cohere's official platform.

Gomez specifically named Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei while pointing to what he describes as a coordinated push by two or three market-leading firms to create an industry-governed standards body. The critical issue, Gomez argues, centers on these companies seeking narrow antitrust exemptions from governments that would effectively legalize their coordination efforts.

The Competition Concern

The Cohere executive contends that such an arrangement would systematically disadvantage smaller competitors and newer entrants to the artificial intelligence market. By establishing rules through a cartel-like structure, Gomez suggests, established players could entrench their positions while making it substantially harder for other firms to compete on equal footing.

This dispute reflects broader tensions within the rapidly consolidating AI industry. As capabilities have concentrated among a small number of well-funded organizations, questions about governance and standard-setting have become increasingly contentious. The distinction between legitimate industry coordination and anticompetitive behavior remains contested, particularly as regulators worldwide examine whether dominant AI companies are leveraging their market power to shape rules in their favor.

Regulatory Implications

Gomez's public criticism highlights a fundamental challenge facing AI governance: who should write the rules for an industry that itself is dramatically reshaping multiple economic sectors. His objections suggest that permitting market leaders to collectively establish standards poses risks beyond typical cartel concerns, potentially allowing them to define safety requirements, interoperability protocols, and other technical frameworks in ways that entrench their dominance.

  • Industry-controlled standards bodies may benefit incumbent players disproportionately
  • Antitrust exemptions could enable coordination that would normally violate competition law
  • Smaller AI companies argue they lack meaningful input in standard-setting discussions
  • Regulators face pressure to clarify which AI governance approaches they will permit

The dispute also underscores how AI policy debates have become increasingly adversarial within the industry itself. Rather than presenting a unified front to policymakers, companies like Cohere are now publicly challenging the approaches favored by larger competitors. This fragmentation could complicate efforts to develop internationally coherent AI governance frameworks.

Looking Ahead

Gomez's intervention suggests that questions about AI industry structure and competitive dynamics will continue generating friction among market participants. Regulators examining AI market concentration, particularly in the United States and Europe, will likely view these internal disputes as relevant to their own investigations into whether dominant firms are abusing their positions.

The outcome of these governance debates could significantly influence the trajectory of AI development and deployment. If standard-setting bodies do gain influence over how AI systems are built and deployed, the composition and decision-making processes of those bodies will directly affect which approaches gain acceptance and which companies can participate meaningfully in shaping AI's future.
