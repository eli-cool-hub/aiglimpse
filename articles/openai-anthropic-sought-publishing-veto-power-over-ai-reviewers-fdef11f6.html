OpenAI, Anthropic Sought Publishing Veto Power Over AI Reviewers

Major AI labs attempted to restrict independent evaluators' ability to publish findings, raising questions about transparency and competitive pressures in the industry.

By AI Glimpse Newsroom
·
October 13, 2026

Two of the AI industry's largest companies pushed to gain substantial control over what independent researchers can publish when evaluating their systems, according to reporting on recent negotiations between the labs and outside auditors.

According to Reuters via The Next Web, Anthropic and OpenAI both sought approval rights over published findings from third-party evaluators, citing concerns that detailed results could expose proprietary methods and trade secrets. The companies expressed particular anxiety about reviewers with direct access to internal systems who might share sensitive details with competitors.

The Approval Push

Through at least October 5, both firms advocated for restrictive publication controls. The requests persisted into early October before discussions apparently shifted course. The proposal would have given each company significant leverage over the final form and content of independent assessments, potentially limiting what the public learns about these increasingly powerful AI systems.

The push reflects growing tensions between commercial AI developers and the broader ecosystem of researchers, auditors, and watchdogs seeking to evaluate these technologies. As AI models become more capable and their deployment more widespread, questions about their safety, accuracy, and potential harms have intensified.

Why Transparency Matters

Independent evaluation serves a critical function in the AI field. Outside researchers can identify issues that internal teams might overlook or downplay. When companies evaluate their own systems, conflicts of interest inevitably arise. Third-party audits provide crucial credibility and accountability in an industry still developing governance standards.

Restrictions on publication could undermine this verification process. If evaluators cannot freely report their findings, the public loses visibility into how well these systems actually perform, what biases they exhibit, and what risks they pose. This matters beyond technical circles: policymakers, journalists, and civil society groups rely on independent assessments to make informed decisions about AI regulation and corporate responsibility.

Industry Pressures

The attempted restrictions also reflect competitive dynamics within the AI industry. Both companies are racing to develop and deploy advanced models ahead of rivals. Revealing technical vulnerabilities or methodological breakthroughs could create business disadvantages. Yet this competitive reality collides with legitimate public interests in understanding these technologies.

The situation mirrors broader debates about corporate transparency. Tech companies routinely assert that publishing detailed security research could aid malicious actors. The AI industry makes similar arguments: detailed capability assessments or vulnerability disclosures might accelerate development by competitors or enable misuse. These concerns are not baseless, but they can also justify excessive secrecy that impedes legitimate oversight.

What Comes Next

The apparent shift in these companies' positions by October 8 suggests negotiations produced some compromise, though details remain unclear. How this evolves will shape the future of independent AI evaluation and what the public can know about systems increasingly embedded in consequential applications.

The episode underscores an emerging challenge for AI governance: balancing legitimate business interests against democratic needs for transparency and accountability. As these systems grow more powerful and their use more widespread, this tension will only intensify.
