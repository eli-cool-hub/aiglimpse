UK Courts Ban Meta Smart Glasses, Citing Recording Risks

England and Wales prohibit wearable devices in courtrooms while maintaining phone restrictions, raising questions about AI-enabled surveillance in legal settings.

By AI Glimpse Newsroom
·
August 12, 2026

England and Wales have instituted a formal ban on Meta smart glasses inside courtroom facilities, marking an escalation in how judicial systems are regulating artificial intelligence-enabled wearable technology. His Majesty's Courts and Tribunals Service announced the policy will apply across criminal, civil, and family court proceedings, with confiscation of devices at entry points and return upon exit, according to AI Weekly reporting.

The restriction targets what judges view as a particularly acute threat: eyewear equipped with integrated cameras and AI processing capabilities that enable seamless, inconspicuous recording. Unlike smartphones, which courts have historically managed through honor systems asking users not to document proceedings, wearable devices present novel enforcement challenges.

Why Smart Glasses Pose a Different Problem

The policy distinction between phones and smart eyewear reveals fundamental concerns about how AI-powered devices change courtroom dynamics. Smartphones remain permitted under the assumption users will comply with recording prohibitions, yet Meta's glasses incorporate always-on cameras paired with machine learning systems that can recognize faces, log audio, and transmit data remotely.

This asymmetry in treatment reflects broader anxieties about ambient AI. While a person holding a phone is obviously documenting a proceeding, someone wearing smart glasses presents no visible indication they are capturing sensitive judicial proceedings. The device's AI capabilities compound these concerns: built-in computer vision systems can automatically identify individuals, extract key statements, and archive information without requiring active user input.

Broader Implications for AI Regulation

The UK courtroom ban signals how institutions are beginning to address governance gaps created by AI-equipped wearables. Unlike established camera restrictions, which date back decades, policies regulating smart glasses remain nascent across most legal systems. This move by the English and Welsh judiciary may establish a template for other common-law jurisdictions wrestling with similar questions.

  • Enforcement challenges: Courts must develop screening procedures for devices indistinguishable from ordinary eyewear
  • Technology specificity: The ban targets Meta hardware rather than establishing category-wide restrictions on all smart eyewear
  • Privacy protection: Judicial proceedings involve sensitive testimony that statutory law has long protected from public recording

The measure also reflects tension between innovation and institutional security. As wearable AI becomes more sophisticated and commercially prevalent, courts face pressure to either embrace new technologies or actively exclude them. The Welsh and English approach suggests preference for exclusion at least until regulatory frameworks mature.

What This Means for Meta and the Industry

For Meta, the restriction represents a concrete commercial limitation on its smartglasses expansion, particularly as the company markets the devices for their recording and documentation capabilities. However, the ban remains geographically limited, affecting only UK courtroom access rather than broader circulation or sale.

The policy also creates incentives for regulators elsewhere to establish their own standards before courts resort to blanket prohibitions. Technology companies might accelerate development of privacy-preserving features, geofencing systems, or consent mechanisms that could satisfy institutional concerns while preserving device functionality.

The distinction between how courts treat phones and smart glasses underscores how AI integration changes regulatory calculus, forcing legal systems to make technology-specific decisions rather than relying on device-agnostic principles.
