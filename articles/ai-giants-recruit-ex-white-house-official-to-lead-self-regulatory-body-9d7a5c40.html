AI Giants Recruit Ex-White House Official to Lead Self-Regulatory Body

Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic tap Sriram Krishnan to head industry safety standards group, marking a pivot in regulatory strategy.

By AI Glimpse Newsroom
·
September 24, 2026

Three of the world's most influential artificial intelligence companies are consolidating their power over industry governance by recruiting a former Trump administration official to oversee their shared safety standards. According to AI Weekly, Google, OpenAI, and Anthropic have extended offers to Sriram Krishnan, who recently served as Senior White House Policy Advisor on Artificial Intelligence, to lead what the group is calling the Frontier AI Standards Agency.

The move represents a striking reversal of position from Krishnan's tenure in government. During his time in the Trump administration starting in January 2025, he actively discouraged federal authorities from establishing independent regulatory frameworks for advanced AI systems. Now, the major labs are asking him to architect the very kind of self-policing mechanism that would sidestep traditional government oversight.

Industry Self-Regulation Takes Shape

The proposed agency signals how leading AI developers increasingly view themselves as capable architects of their own compliance infrastructure. Rather than waiting for lawmakers to establish binding rules, the three firms appear intent on creating internal standards that could serve as a de facto regulatory system for the frontier AI sector.

This approach offers several potential advantages for the companies involved. A standards body controlled by industry participants allows rapid iteration on safety guidelines without navigating the slow machinery of legislative processes. It also positions these firms as responsible actors invested in managing risks associated with their technologies.

However, the arrangement raises immediate questions about accountability and conflicts of interest. An organization led by three of the largest players in AI development might prioritize business-friendly rules over more stringent safety measures.

Strategic Implications

Krishnan's recruitment underscores the sophisticated political strategy these companies are pursuing. His credentials bridge the gap between Silicon Valley and Washington, lending credibility to what might otherwise appear as corporate self-interest. His prior resistance to government regulation makes his involvement particularly notable: it suggests the standards body could operate with minimal friction from federal authorities.

  • The agency would develop technical and operational standards for frontier AI systems
  • Participating companies commit to adhering to shared safety benchmarks
  • The structure aims to preempt more restrictive government mandates
  • Krishnan's prior policy experience could help align the effort with government expectations

Broader Industry Context

This development reflects intensifying competition over how AI governance should function globally. While the European Union has implemented its AI Act with regulatory teeth, and various governments explore different oversight models, major American labs have largely resisted external constraints. Creating their own standards body allows these companies to shape the narrative around responsible AI development while maintaining operational flexibility.

The success of this venture depends significantly on external perception. If independent researchers, civil society groups, and government officials view the standards agency as genuinely independent and rigorous, it could set a template for industry self-governance. If it appears captured by corporate interests, it risks becoming a public relations exercise that attracts regulatory backlash.

Krishnan's appointment will likely face scrutiny from policymakers concerned about regulatory capture. His nomination essentially asks Washington to trust that someone who opposed government regulation while in office will now oversee an industry body with meaningful constraints on the companies that recruited him.
